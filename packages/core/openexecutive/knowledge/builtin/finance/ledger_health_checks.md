# Is Anything Wrong in the Books?

Some faults in a set of books don't show on any everyday screen. An entry that doesn't balance, a sub-ledger that has drifted from its control account, stock held at no value: each one sits quietly until an audit, a migration or a report that disagrees with another brings it out. Checking for them is routine work, and so is saying plainly when nothing is wrong.

## The Checks

**Entries that don't balance, or shouldn't be there.** Every journal, invoice and payment should post equal debits and credits, in the company's currency, to accounts that exist and are open, and the trial balance's debits and credits should agree. A ledger row whose document was cancelled or never submitted, or one that posts to a disabled or group account, is invalid. Most accounting systems have a report of invalid ledger entries; run it for the period.

**Ledgers that disagree.** The detail and the summary should agree:
- the **payment ledger** (what each customer or supplier owes, invoice by invoice) against the **general ledger's** receivable and payable accounts, by party;
- **stock** in the stock ledger against the stock accounts in the general ledger;
- **fixed assets** in the asset register against the asset accounts.

Any difference is worth a look, and the parties or accounts where it sits are the place to start.

**Stock without a value, or below zero.** An item received at no rate, or valued at zero, makes the stock balance and the cost of goods sold wrong. Negative stock (more issued than was ever received) means a receipt is missing or was posted late.

**Duplicates.** The same supplier invoice or customer payment entered twice: same party, same amount, close dates, or a reference number that differs by a typo.

**Opening balances.** After a migration, the opening entries should add up to the old system's closing trial balance, and the temporary opening account should be back to zero.

## How to Check

1. **Fix the company and the period.** Say which. Some faults only matter for a closed period, others for today.
2. **Run each check** from the books' own reports, not by adding up records by hand.
3. **Record each result,** clean or not, with the figure that shows it: the report it came from, the company, the currency and the period.
4. **For each fault,** give the entries or parties involved and the size of the difference.

## Saying What Was Checked

An empty result reads like "didn't look" unless the answer says what was looked at. So the answer lists every check with its result, for example:

- invalid ledger entries, October: none;
- payment ledger against general ledger, receivables and payables, as of October 31: they agree;
- stock at zero value or below zero, today: none.

"Nothing is wrong" is a finding when the checks behind it are named.

## Who Acts

Correcting a ledger fault is a bookkeeping job: whoever keeps the books investigates and posts the correction, and whoever signs off the period approves it if the period is closed. How to treat something for tax, or whether a correction changes what was filed, is for the company's accountant or tax adviser.

## What It Is Not

- It doesn't post, cancel or correct anything. A person does that, in the system that holds the books.
- A difference isn't always an error. Timing (a payment posted after the period end) explains many, and the answer says so when the figures show it.
