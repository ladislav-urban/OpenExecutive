# A 13-Week Cash Outlook from Today's Records

A trading business rarely runs out of profit; it runs out of cash in a particular week. A customer pays late, a supplier's terms end, payroll and a tax payment fall in the same fortnight. A 13-week cash outlook shows those weeks before they arrive. It is built from what the books already hold, week by week, so every line can be traced to a document or a stated assumption.

It is an **outlook from today's records**. It says what happens to cash if invoices are paid when the stated assumptions say they will be, and nothing more. When an assumption changes, the outlook changes with it.

## What It Is For

- **Seeing the low point.** The lowest closing balance in the next quarter, and the week it falls in, is the number most worth knowing.
- **Timing, not totals.** A profitable quarter can still have a week where cash goes below what the business must keep in the bank.
- **Giving people time to decide.** A shortfall seen eight weeks out leaves options (chase, delay, draw on a facility, move a purchase). One seen on the day leaves almost none.

It is not a budget, and not a long-range plan. Thirteen weeks is short enough to build from documents that already exist.

## Building It, Week by Week

Use calendar weeks, starting with the current one. For each week:

**Opening cash** is the previous week's closing cash. Week 1 opens with today's balance across the company's bank and cash accounts, in one currency, with the date it was read and the report it came from. That is the balance in the books: if the bank statement hasn't been reconciled to them, say so, because payments the books don't yet show change it.

**Cash in:**
- **Open customer invoices,** placed in the week they are expected to be paid. Start from each invoice's due date, then apply what is known about that customer: if they have paid 30 days late all year, place it 30 days after its due date and say so.
- **Orders delivered or confirmed but not yet invoiced,** only where the terms and timing are clear. Mark these separately: they depend on an invoice that does not exist yet.
- **Other receipts already agreed:** a refund owed to the company, a loan drawdown that has been signed, a grant with a payment date.

**Cash out:**
- **Open supplier invoices,** in the week they are due, or the week the company normally pays them. Where a supplier offers an early-payment discount, show both dates and the amount saved.
- **Purchase orders already placed** but not yet invoiced, in the week the invoice is expected and then paid.
- **Recurring payments:** payroll, rent, loan repayments, subscriptions, from the journals or schedules they recur in.
- **Tax payments** with their filing dates (sales tax or VAT collected, payroll taxes, instalments of income tax).

**Closing cash** = opening cash + cash in − cash out.

Add one line underneath: the **minimum cash** the business keeps in the bank, if it has set one. Every week where closing cash falls below it is a week to look at.

## Show the Assumptions

Every figure that is not a dated document is an assumption, and each one goes in a list beside the outlook:

- which customers are assumed to pay late, and by how many days, and why (their payment history this year);
- which uninvoiced orders are included, and when they are assumed to be billed;
- recurring items and the schedule they come from;
- anything left out on purpose (a disputed invoice, a sale not yet confirmed).

A person reading the outlook should be able to change any assumption and see what moves.

## Reading It

- **The lowest closing balance and its week.** State it plainly: "Cash is lowest in week 7, at 18,400, below the 25,000 kept as a minimum."
- **What drives that week.** Usually one or two items: a large customer paying late, a supplier batch due together, payroll and tax in the same week.
- **How sensitive it is.** Recompute the low point with the largest late payer one week earlier and one week later. If one customer decides the quarter, say so.
- **What it leaves out.** Sales not yet made are not in it. A business with steady new sales will usually do better than its outlook, so read it as a floor.

## Options and Who Decides

The outlook explains the position; people decide what to do about it. The usual options, each shown with what it does to the low point:

| Option | What it changes in the outlook | Usually decided by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chase a late customer, or offer a discount for paying now | Moves an expected receipt earlier | Whoever owns collections or customer credit |
| Pay a supplier later, within terms | Moves a payment later | Whoever approves supplier payments |
| Pay a supplier early for a discount | Saves money, moves cash out earlier | Whoever approves supplier payments |
| Delay a purchase not yet ordered | Removes a future payment | The person whose spend limit covers it |
| Draw on an overdraft or credit facility | Adds cash in, adds cost | The owner, with the lender's terms |
| Change a customer's credit terms | Changes future receipts | Whoever holds customer credit |

Name the person whose role and approval limit covers each choice. A financing decision (borrowing, drawing on a facility) is the owner's, with an accountant or the lender where needed. The outlook does not choose between the options.

## Keeping It Current

Rebuild it weekly from the books, not by editing last week's copy: paid invoices drop out, new ones come in, and the week rolls forward. Compare last week's outlook for this week with what actually happened; where they differ, the assumption behind it was wrong and should change.
