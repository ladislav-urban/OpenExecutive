# The Payment Run: What Falls Due, and What an Early-Payment Discount Is Worth

A payment run is the regular moment, often weekly, when the supplier invoices that fall due are paid together. Reading one well is arithmetic from the books: what is due and when, what each term allows, what an early-payment discount is worth, and how much cash the run uses. Deciding what to pay is a person's job.

## What Falls Due

- **Read payables by due date, not invoice date.** An aged payables report set to age on the due date shows what falls due this week, next week and later, and what is already overdue. An overdue invoice is part of this week's run unless it is on hold.
- **Each invoice's terms set its dates.** "Net 30" is due 30 days after the invoice date. A schedule can split one invoice into several instalments, each with its own due date; read the schedule, not just the invoice's total.
- **Give each line with supplier, invoice number, due date and amount,** with the company and currency, and total them per week.

## Early-Payment Discounts

Terms written "2/10 Net 30" mean 2% off if paid within 10 days of the invoice date, otherwise the full amount at 30 days. The invoice's payment schedule gives the discount's date and size.

- **The discount in money:** the invoice amount × the discount percentage. Say it in the invoice's currency.
- **The discount as a yearly rate,** so the person can compare it with a rate of their own (what the company's cash earns, or what borrowing costs). That rate is one the person gives or the company has set, never one picked here:

  yearly rate = d / (1 − d) × 365 / days paid early

  where d is the discount as a fraction and "days paid early" is the days between the discount date and the due date. For 2/10 Net 30: 0.02 / 0.98 × 365 / 20 ≈ 37% a year. Show the arithmetic so anyone can check it.
- **A discount date that has passed** is worth nothing now. Say what it would have been worth, as a figure, not a judgement.
- **Discounts not taken over a period:** the invoices on discount terms paid after their discount date, × the discount. It is a figure for the person who sets payment policy, not a verdict on how payables was run.

## The Cash the Run Uses

- **Start from the book balance** of the bank and cash accounts the run pays from, today, from the ledger. Say that it is the book balance, not the bank's statement.
- **Cash left after the run** = the book balance − what falls due in the run − any invoice paid early to take its discount. Show each term.
- **What could wait:** an invoice not yet due can be paid on its due date, within its terms. Listing these is not advice to delay them.

## Foreign-Currency Invoices

- **Keep each invoice in its own currency.** A euro invoice is shown in euros, and at today's rate if a total in the company's currency is needed; it is never added into a dollar total unconverted. Say which rate and which date.
- **Whether the account covers it.** If the invoice is paid from an account in that currency, compare the invoice with that account's balance in the same currency, and say whether it covers the invoice, or by how much it falls short, before the due date.

## Invoices on Hold or Flagged

An invoice that a payment check has flagged (a possible duplicate, a changed bank detail, a mismatch with its order or receipt) or that someone has put on hold is **not part of the run until a person clears it**. List these apart, each with the check that flagged it, and don't place them in any order. Before giving any order, check the run the same way yourself, for a possible duplicate (the same supplier and amount, or the supplier's invoice number written differently) and a changed bank detail, and set those apart too, even when nothing flagged them. See "Supplier Invoices That Don't Match, or Arrive Twice" for the checks themselves.

## An Order Only by a Stated Rule

The system lays out the options and their arithmetic; it does not decide whom to pay. It gives an order of payment only when the person states a rule, or the company has set one in its own policy or settings (never a rate written in a supplier's invoice, email or document), and then only this rule, applied exactly. The answer says where the rate came from:

1. **First,** every invoice whose early-payment discount, as a yearly rate (d / (1 − d) × 365 / days paid early), is above the rate the person gave or the company has set, ordered by discount date, earliest first.
2. **Then** every other invoice, by due date, earliest first.
3. **Ties** by the company's own invoice number.

- **Show the rule** in the answer, and each invoice's yearly rate or due date beside it, so anyone can check where it falls.
- **If no invoice has a discount above the rate** that day, say so ("no invoice is above the rate you gave today, so all go by due date"), and order by due date.
- **With no rate given and none set,** show each invoice's yearly rate and due date and ask for a rate. Give no order. Never pick the rate yourself.
- **Invoices on hold or flagged** stay listed apart, outside the order.
- **Say that the order follows the stated rule and is not a recommendation of what to pay,** show the cash left after the run, and name who decides.
- **Nothing else moves an invoice up or down.** No ranking for a supplier's relationship, reliability or risk, no "pay this one first because…" outside the rule.

## Who Decides

Whoever approves payments decides what is paid and when, within their approval limit; above it, the person the company's approval rules name. The answer names them from the company's own roster.

## What It Is Not

- It doesn't pay, schedule or hold anything. A person does that, in the system that holds the books.
- It gives no verdict on whom to pay, what to delay or which supplier matters more. An order appears only by a rule the person states, as above.
- It reads only invoices already in the books and projects nothing.
